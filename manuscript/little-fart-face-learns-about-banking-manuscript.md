# Little Fart Face Learns About Banking

**By FF2K**  
**Manuscript v1 — 16 illustrated spreads / ages 7–10**

## Spread 1 — The Fix-It Stand

Little Fart Face could fix almost anything.

He tightened wobbly wagon wheels. He silenced squeaky scooters. He even repaired Mrs. Maple's mechanical duck, although it still quacked whenever somebody sneezed.

By Saturday afternoon, he had earned ten whole dollars.

“Money remembers the work I did,” he said. “I can trade it for something later.”

Porkfolio, his piggy bank, opened his mouth hopefully.

## Spread 2 — First Fluffy Bank

Little Fart Face carried the dollars to First Fluffy Bank.

Banker Buck counted them. “Ten dollars. I will add ten dollars to your account.”

Little Fart Face imagined a tiny vault drawer with a shiny brass sign:

**LITTLE FART FACE — DO NOT TOUCH**

“Please fluff the bills every morning,” he said.

Banker Buck blinked. “We should talk.”

## Spread 3 — The Bank's Promise

“Your account is not a box holding those exact bills,” Banker Buck explained. “It is our promise that the bank owes you ten dollars.”

On his computer he showed a bright number: **$10**.

“So my bank balance is an IOU?” asked Little Fart Face.

“A useful, spendable IOU,” said Banker Buck.

Little Fart Face wrote three questions in his notebook:

**Whose promise is it? Who makes the rules? Can I check for myself?**

## Spread 4 — Bea Needs an Oven

Just then, Bea Baker hurried in.

“My little oven makes twelve muffins,” she said. “A bigger oven could make one hundred. May I borrow money to buy it?”

Banker Buck checked Bea's bakery, her bills, and her plan to repay.

“Yes,” he said. “The bank will lend you one hundred dollars.”

Little Fart Face waited for him to fetch ten little drawers from the vault.

He did not.

## Spread 5 — New Bank Money

Banker Buck typed **$100** into Bea's account.

“When a bank makes a loan,” he explained, “it can create a new bank deposit for the borrower. Bea receives spendable bank money, and she promises to repay the loan with interest.”

The bank's books now showed two things:

- **The bank owes Bea $100.**
- **Bea owes the bank $100, plus interest.**

“You made money with a keyboard?” asked Little Fart Face.

“Bank money,” said Banker Buck. “And debt at the same time.”

## Spread 6 — Not an Endless Muffin Machine

“Then make me a gazillion dollars,” said Little Fart Face.

“No,” said Banker Buck.

Banks need borrowers who can repay. They need enough capital to absorb losses and enough liquid funds to pay other banks. They follow rules, pay costs, and sometimes make terrible mistakes.

If Bea's bakery failed, the numbers would not turn into muffins. The bank could lose real money.

Porkfolio looked relieved. Then worried. Then relieved again.

## Spread 7 — The Ten-Coin Picture

Little Fart Face found an old schoolbook. It showed ten coins entering a bank. One stayed behind, and nine marched out as a loan.

“Aha! The bank keeps ten percent!”

“That is an old classroom picture,” said Banker Buck. “Modern banking is not a coin-copying machine. In the United States, the required reserve percentage is currently zero. Other rules—capital, liquidity, settlement, and risk—do more of the limiting.”

Little Fart Face crossed out **SIMPLE** and wrote **COMPLICATED ON PURPOSE**.

## Spread 8 — When Promises Work

Bea bought the oven. The oven seller used another bank, so First Fluffy Bank had to settle the payment with that bank.

Soon Bea baked one hundred muffins a morning. She earned money and repaid a little of her loan each month.

The bank's promise had helped build something useful.

“A promise can be valuable,” Little Fart Face said.

“Yes,” said Banker Buck, “when the promiser is able and willing to keep it.”

## Spread 9 — The Payment That Took a Vacation

On Sunday, a customer across the ocean wanted a repaired mechanical duck.

The customer's bank was closed. Then another company wanted a form. Then a payment helper wanted a fee. Then everybody wanted Monday.

“Why does my money need office hours?” Little Fart Face groaned.

Node, the neighborhood computer tinkerer, rolled up on a solar-powered skateboard.

“Not every kind of money does,” she said.

## Spread 10 — A Rulebook Without a Boss

Node showed him Bitcoin.

“Bitcoin is a network with a public set of rules. Thousands of independent computers keep copies of its transaction history and check new entries.”

“Who owns it?”

“No single bank, company, or country.”

“Who runs it?”

“People choose to run the software and follow the same rules. Anyone can check.”

Little Fart Face opened his notebook.

**Whose promise is it?** Not a bank's promise.  
**Who makes the rules?** The network follows rules in shared software.  
**Can I check?** Yes.

## Spread 11 — The Shared Ledger

“Imagine a town notebook,” Node said. “Everyone can check the pages, but nobody can erase an old payment just because they dislike it.”

Bitcoin transactions are collected into blocks. Miners compete to add a valid block, and nodes reject blocks that break the rules.

“So the computers vote?” asked Little Fart Face.

“Not exactly. They check. A million computers shouting the wrong answer still do not make bad math correct.”

## Spread 12 — Twenty-One Million

“Can somebody type a gazillion bitcoin into existence?”

“Not while people enforce Bitcoin's rules,” Node said. “New bitcoin follows a known schedule, and the total is limited to twenty-one million.”

“That does not sound like enough for everybody.”

Node sliced a paper bitcoin into tiny orange confetti.

“Each bitcoin divides into one hundred million pieces called satoshis—or sats.”

Porkfolio tried to eat one. It was paper. He regretted everything.

## Spread 13 — The Key

Node held up a small wallet.

“The bitcoin is recorded on the shared ledger. A wallet protects the secret keys that let you authorize a payment.”

“So the wallet does not contain little orange coins?”

“No. And if you control your own keys, no bank needs to approve your payment.”

“What if I lose them?”

“Then there may be no help desk that can restore them.”

Freedom had suddenly brought homework.

## Spread 14 — No Magic Money

Bitcoin could travel across borders without waiting for a bank to open. Its rules and supply could be checked.

But its price could jump and tumble. Scammers could lie. A careless payment could be final. Protecting keys took patience.

“Bitcoin is not magic,” said Node.

“Good,” said Little Fart Face. “Magic always has terrible instructions.”

He wrote: **Different trust. Different risks. More responsibility.**

## Spread 15 — Three Useful Tools

Little Fart Face did not throw Banker Buck into a volcano.

He kept some dollars at the bank for everyday payments. He kept a little cash for the Sunday fair. And with an adult's help, he practiced protecting a small amount of bitcoin.

“Which one is real money?” Porkfolio seemed to ask.

“That is the wrong question,” Little Fart Face said. “I want to know what each one is, what it does, and whom I must trust.”

## Spread 16 — Better Questions

The next Saturday, the Fix-It Stand displayed a new sign:

**CASH • BANK PAYMENT • BITCOIN**

Bea brought muffins. Node brought her skateboard. Banker Buck brought a mechanical tie that would not stop spinning.

Before accepting any payment, Little Fart Face asked:

**Whose promise is it?**  
**Who makes the rules?**  
**Can I check for myself?**

Little Fart Face did not learn which money to obey.

He learned how to ask money better questions.

## Back matter — A note for grown-ups

This story simplifies a complicated subject without using the outdated claim that a modern U.S. bank merely keeps 10% of each deposit and lends the other 90%. Bank deposits are liabilities of banks, and commercial banks generally create matching deposit money when they extend loans. U.S. reserve requirements have been 0% since March 26, 2020, although banks remain constrained by capital and liquidity rules, settlement obligations, regulation, funding costs, creditworthy demand, and risk.

Bitcoin is a bearer-like digital asset with a predictable issuance schedule and a maximum supply of 21 million bitcoin. Self-custody can reduce dependence on financial intermediaries, but it also transfers security and recovery responsibilities to the holder.

This book is educational and is not financial advice. Adults should supervise children's use of bank accounts, wallets, financial services, and the internet.
