# Little Fart Face Learns About Banking

**Status:** Story blueprint / factual foundation  
**Author:** FF2K  
**Target reader:** Ages 7–10, with an adult reading along  
**Format:** 32-page illustrated picture book, approximately 1,100–1,400 words

## The promise

Little Fart Face puts ten hard-earned dollars into a bank and assumes the banker locks those exact dollars in a little box. He soon learns that a bank deposit is really an IOU: the bank owes him money, while using its balance sheet to make loans and create new bank deposits. That system can help people build homes and businesses, but it depends on rules, good judgment, and trust.

Then Little Fart Face discovers a different kind of money: Bitcoin. Nobody owns the Bitcoin network, its rules can be checked by anyone, its supply is limited, and a person can hold the keys to their own money. Bitcoin has tradeoffs too: keys can be lost, prices move, transactions are final, and self-custody requires responsibility.

The lesson is not “banks evil, Bitcoin magic.” It is: **know whose promise you hold, know the rules, and choose the right tool.**

## Factual guardrail

Do not teach the familiar “the bank keeps 10% and lends the other 90%” story as present-day fact.

- A bank deposit is a liability of the bank—money the bank owes its customer.
- Modern commercial banks create new deposit money when they make loans; they do not simply hand a borrower the same physical dollars another customer deposited.
- In the United States, the Federal Reserve's reserve requirement has been 0% since March 26, 2020.
- Banks are still constrained by capital, liquidity, settlement needs, regulation, borrower demand, credit risk, and the possibility of losses.
- Bitcoin has a predictable issuance schedule and a maximum supply of 21 million bitcoin.

Kid-level phrasing:

> “The bank doesn't put your exact dollars in a box. It writes you a promise: ‘We owe you this much.’ When it approves a loan, it can write a new spendable bank balance for the borrower. Banks cannot do this forever—rules, costs, risks, and other banks keep them in check—but the number in your account is a bank promise, not a bag with your name on it.”

## Original story engine

Rabbi Michael Caras's *Bitcoin Money: A Tale of Bitville Discovering Good Money* uses a town and a lemonade stand to let children discover the qualities of money through a practical problem. Our book should borrow only that broad educational principle: **show the problem through a child's small business.** It must use original characters, setting, dialogue, scenes, and metaphors.

Little Fart Face runs a weekend **Fart Face Fix-It Stand**, repairing wagons, scooters, and squeaky toys. The bank story begins when he deposits his earnings. The Bitcoin story begins when two customers want to pay him without a bank: one lives across town, and one lives across the ocean.

## Recurring refrain

> “Whose promise is it? Who makes the rules? Can I check for myself?”

This appears three times: with cash, a bank balance, and Bitcoin.

## 32-page map

### Pages 1–2 — Title spread

Little Fart Face stands outside First Fluffy Bank with ten dollars and enormous confidence.

### Pages 3–4 — Earning money

At his Fix-It Stand, Little Fart Face earns ten dollars by repairing three neighborhood toys. Money is introduced as a tool for remembering work and trading it later.

### Pages 5–6 — The deposit

Banker Buck takes the ten dollars. Little Fart Face imagines a tiny vault drawer labeled “FART FACE—DO NOT TOUCH.”

### Pages 7–8 — The first surprise

Banker Buck explains that the account balance is the bank's promise to pay Little Fart Face when he asks. His exact bills are not sitting in a personal drawer.

### Pages 9–10 — Where loans come from

Neighbor Bea needs money for a new bakery oven. The bank checks whether she can repay, approves a loan, and adds a new balance to her account. A clear two-column picture shows the bank now has a loan asset and a matching deposit liability.

### Pages 11–12 — Not a magic trick

The bank cannot create endless loans. It needs trustworthy borrowers, enough capital and liquid funds, and the ability to settle payments with other banks. Bad loans can hurt or close a bank.

### Pages 13–14 — The old “10%” classroom story

Little Fart Face finds an old textbook showing ten coins in and nine coins out. Banker Buck says it is a useful historical cartoon, not how today's U.S. reserve rules work. The current reserve requirement is zero, but other safety rules still apply.

### Pages 15–16 — What trust means

Most days, bank promises are convenient: cards work, bills get paid, and insured deposits have protections. But customers must trust the bank, its computers, its rules, and the institutions behind it.

### Pages 17–18 — A payment that gets stuck

A customer far away wants a repaired toy, but the payment is delayed because the banks are closed and several helpers must approve the transfer. Little Fart Face asks whether money can move without asking one company for permission.

### Pages 19–20 — Meeting Bitcoin

Node, a cheerful neighborhood computer nerd, introduces Bitcoin as a shared public rulebook copied by many independent computers. No single computer is the boss.

### Pages 21–22 — The ledger

Transactions are grouped and added to a history that the network checks. The illustration uses a chain of transparent wagons carrying sealed transaction blocks—without claiming that coins literally travel inside blocks.

### Pages 23–24 — Scarcity

Bitcoin's rules limit the total supply to 21 million. New bitcoin follows a known schedule rather than one person deciding to make more. Explain that each bitcoin can be divided into 100 million satoshis.

### Pages 25–26 — Keys, not accounts

A wallet holds keys, not coins. The keys let Little Fart Face authorize spending. If he controls the keys, he does not need a bank's permission—but nobody can replace a lost secret for him.

### Pages 27–28 — Honest tradeoffs

Bitcoin can move globally and be self-custodied, but the price changes, mistakes can be final, scams exist, and safe key storage takes care. “Different” does not mean “effortless.”

### Pages 29–30 — Choosing tools

Little Fart Face keeps some spending money at the bank, cash for a neighborhood fair, and a small amount of bitcoin he learns to secure. He understands what each tool does and whom he must trust.

### Pages 31–32 — Final lesson

At the Fix-It Stand, a sign offers CASH, BANK, or BITCOIN. Little Fart Face repeats:

> “Whose promise is it? Who makes the rules? Can I check for myself?”

Final line:

> Little Fart Face did not learn which money to obey. He learned how to ask money better questions.

## Character roster

- **Little Fart Face:** curious, mechanically minded, skeptical without being cynical.
- **Banker Buck:** friendly and honest; explains banking rather than serving as a cartoon villain.
- **Bea Baker:** demonstrates productive credit by borrowing for an oven.
- **Node:** a kid inventor who explains Bitcoin using tools and shared rulebooks.
- **Porkfolio:** Little Fart Face's anxious piggy bank; silent visual comic relief.

## Back matter

### Words grown-ups pretend they understand

Money, cash, bank deposit, loan, interest, bank capital, liquidity, settlement, ledger, Bitcoin, bitcoin, satoshi, wallet, private key, node, miner, block, confirmation, self-custody.

### Three questions for every kind of money

1. Is this an object, somebody's promise, or an entry in a shared ledger?
2. Who can change the rules or stop a payment?
3. What happens if the keeper fails—or if I lose my key?

### Parent note

This book is educational, not financial advice. Adults should supervise any child's use of financial accounts, Bitcoin wallets, or online services.
